### A Formal Definition of Spatial Thinking
The NRC (2006) defined spatial thinking as an amalgam of three interrelated cognitive skills. 

1. **Concepts of Space:** Concepts of space are the building blocks of spatial thinking. These are properties like location, distance, direction, pattern, distribution, and network that allow us to describe and reason about the positions, arrangements, and relationships of objects. They range in complexity from fundamental primitives (location, identity, magnitude) through simple concepts built from those primitives (distance, direction, region) to complex concepts assembled from combinations of simpler ones (pattern, hierarchy, spatial association, diffusion). By understanding the meaning of space, we can use its properties as a means of structuring problems, finding answers, and presenting solutions. 

2. **Tools of Representation:** are the external means used to organize, visualize, analyze, and communicate spatial information (e.g., maps, diagrams, graphs, GIS displays). They are not passive illustrations but active reasoning instruments because representational choices shapes what spatial relationships can be seen, what is concealed, and how information is understood.

3. **Processes of Reasoning:** are the cognitive operations applied to spatial concepts and representations that move a person beyond a given set of information. They range from low-level input tasks like identifying and describing (e.g., naming features on a map) through processing tasks like classifying and comparing, to high-level output tasks like generating hypotheses, evaluating alternatives, and creating solutions.

Spatial thinking is not a single ability. Spatial thinking is a collection of skills that can be developed, practiced, and assessed. It operates across disciplines and scales, from molecular biology to global climate systems.

> A critical spatial thinker uses spatial concepts and geographic principles to understand problems, derive solutions, and communicate effectively about geographic processes, patterns, and outcomes, whether they are producing or consuming that information.

### The Three Contexts of Spatial Thinking
Spatial thinking is used in at least three different contexts: 

1. **Cognition _in_ space** — the geography of our life spaces. This is thinking within the everyday world: navigating, wayfinding, reading a transit map, packing a suitcase. GIScience example: a field researcher using a GPS unit to navigate to sample sites.

2. **Cognition _about_ space** — the geography of our physical spaces. This is scientific reasoning about the structure and function of spatial phenomena across scales. GIScience example: analyzing how urban heat islands form through the spatial distribution of impervious surfaces.

3. **Cognition _with_ space** — the geography of our intellectual spaces. This is using spatial structures to reason about concepts that are not inherently spatial—organizing ideas into diagrams, timelines, concept maps, or network visualizations. GIScience example: using a knowledge graph to model relationships between geospatial ontologies.

And serves at least three functions across all of these contexts: 
1. **Descriptive**: capturing and conveying the appearances of and relations among objects (e.g., mapping land cover). 

2. **Analytic**: understanding the structure of objects and phenomena (e.g., spatial pattern analysis). 

3. **Inferential**: generating answers to questions about evolution, behavior, and function (e.g., predicting flood extent from terrain models).

## The Hierarchy of Spatial Concepts (35mins)
### Spatial Concept Categorization
Spatial concepts are the building blocks of spatial thinking. A series of categorization schemes and research studies from the mid-1990s to 2010s attempted to group these concepts according to their complexity and relationships with one another (Golledge 1992, 1995, Golledge et al. 2008, Lobben et al. 2015). This research also attempted to develop educational sequencing to improve spatial thinking skills in K-12 students.  Let's look at Gollege's scheme as it is the most influential. 

Golledge broke spatial concepts into five-levels of increasing complexity. 

| Level | Label                                 | Example Concepts                                                                       | Tasks                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                        |
| ----- | ------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| 1     | Primitives                            | Identity; location; magnitude; time                                                    | Identify objects by type or category; recognize place of objects or features; recognize differences in quantity of occurrences; recognize temporal diffusion over space and time.                                                                                                                                            |
| 2     | Simple (first-order derivatives)      | Arrangements; distribution; line; shape; boundary; distance; reference frame; sequence | Recognize (plan) a path between an origin and destination; determine spatial limits in natural and built environments; recognize spatially-based forms of membership; understand, cognize, and constrain structures.                                                                                                         |
| 3     | Difficult (second-order derivatives)  | Adjacency; angle; classification; coordinate; grid pattern; polygon                    | Recognize closeness in space or find nearest neighbors in a distribution; develop language and means of expressing direction from a location; create schema for uniquely identifying places in spaces; develop an areal referencing procedure; identify arrangement of a distribution; determine areas with irregular edges. |
| 4     | Complicated (third-order derivatives) | Buffer; connectivity; gradient; profile; representation; scale                         | Develop a static or dynamic area around a node; assess type and completeness of interpoint linkages; measure slope between two occurrences with different elevations; create a cross section; create a spatialized way to present data; determine how change is effected by altering the real world representations ratio.   |
| 5     | Complex (fourth-order derivatives)    | Areal association; interpolations; map projection; subjective space; virtual reality   | Measure degree of similarity between point, line, or area distributions; determine value of two or more location/place-based distributions; represent curved surface on a flat sheet of paper; recognize space as usually represented in a memory; comprehend representation (desktop).                                      |

1. **Buffer analysis (Complicated)**: starts with location (primitive), requires distance and proximity (simple), invokes area (difficult). When a buffer is used to assess service area coverage it also invokes pattern and distribution (complex).

Have the students do 
**Overlay analysis**: requires identity and location (primitives), boundary and region (simple), and produces spatial association and pattern (complex).

**Hot spot analysis (Getis-Ord Gi*)**: location and magnitude (primitives) → distance and neighborhood (simple) → spatial autocorrelation and clustering (complex).

## Representation and Reasoning (20mins)
### Activity 2 - Draw a Map of Campus (10mins)
**Purpose:** To illustrate how basic spatial concepts have been identified across domains and in the general public. The to link the Lynch framework back to that of Golledge. 

**Task:** Draw a map of campus.

**Debrief:** Introduce the Lynch framework from the Table below. Then have students make a connection between the Lynch concepts and those from Golledge. 

**Table 1.** Elements of Mental Maps from Lynch (1960)

| Element | Description |
|---|---|
| **Paths** | The channels along which people move (streets, walkways, transit lines, canals). Often the dominant element in people's mental images. |
| **Edges** | Linear boundaries that are not paths; they separate one area from another (shorelines, walls, railroad cuts, edges of development). |
| **Districts** | Medium-to-large sections of the city that people mentally "enter" and recognize as having a common identifying character (a neighborhood, a business district). | 
| **Nodes** | Strategic focal points that people can enter, typically intersections or convergence points (a town square, a major junction, a transit hub). |
| **Landmarks** | External reference points used for orientation but not typically entered; identified by their singularity or contrast with surroundings (a church steeple, a distinctive building, a monument). |
### Comments on Representation (10mins)
**Tools of representation** maps, diagrams, graphs, GIS displays, 3D visualizations are all used to organize, analyze, and communicate spatial information.

Key points to make:

- Representations are not passive illustrations; they are reasoning tools. The choice of representation shapes what you can see and what you miss.
- Different representations reveal different spatial relationships. A choropleth map and a cartogram of the same data tell different spatial stories.
- The highest-order skill in this domain is **representational judgment**: knowing which type of representation best serves a particular purpose, and being able to translate information from one form to another (e.g., map to graph to cross-section).

**GIScience connection:** Graduate students in GIScience will encounter representational choices constantly. Choosing projection, color scheme, classification method, level of aggregation, ... Each choice shapes the spatial narrative communicated to users and carries ethical implications.

## Spatial Reasoning and Cognitive Process (5mins)
The third component is **processes of reasoning**—the cognitive operations applied to spatial concepts and representations. Here we will use Jo & Bednarz (2009) adoption of Costa's (2001) three-level hierarchy:

1. **Input level**: recalling, identifying, describing spatial information without transforming it. (e.g., "What landcover types are shown on this map?")
2. **Processing level**: comparing, classifying, inferring, analyzing. (e.g., "How does the spatial distribution of income relate to access to green space?")
3. **Output level**: evaluating, hypothesizing, predicting, creating solutions. (e.g., "Where should we locate a new facility to maximize equitable access, and what spatial trade-offs does that involve?")

Re-emphasize: the transition from description to inference—from telling what a spatial pattern looks like to explaining why it exists—is the critical developmental threshold for graduate-level spatial thinking.

### Toward Critical Spatial Thinking (4 min)

Briefly introduce the concept of **critical spatial thinking** (Sinton, 2016) as the capstone of spatial reasoning. A critical spatial thinker does not just use spatial concepts and representations—they evaluate them. This involves:

- Outward-facing critique: examining the sources, assumptions, accuracy, and limitations of spatial information and representations.
- Inward-facing reflection: monitoring one's own spatial reasoning processes, recognizing biases and gaps, and adjusting approaches accordingly.

Frame this as a horizon for the course: by the end, students should be able to interrogate the spatial reasoning embedded in their own workflows and in the products they create for others.
